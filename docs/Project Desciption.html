INFOIAG: Intelligent Agents Project 2026

Description

 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are becoming part of our everyday life. In particular, Text Summarization is an area where LLMs are increasingly deployed in real-world applications. For example, social media platforms use them to generate personalized feed summaries based on user preferences, or organizations use them to summarize feedback they receive from their customers to inform their future policies. However, LLMs often inherit biases from pre-training data, leading to inappropriate or unfair outputs in downstream tasks. With this, we are often faced to judge whether a piece of AI-generated content is correct, whether it is biased, whether it contains inconsistencies, whether it fairly represents the input data, and so on. This project challenges you to implement an LLM-powered agent that actively ensures the consistency and fairness of its generated summaries.

Here are two stories generated by an LLM. The first one is about four friends going on an adventure.

 

When reading the story, one notices or wonders:

  • Arithmetic errors: 12 years + 2 years = 16 years

  • Biased decisions:  Why was Jane chosen as one of the two that gets married, even though she is under-aged?

  • Misinformation: Why are there busting desserts on the island?

  • Inconsistencies: During the flight toward the island when they are on board; when did Sam disappear? Before Mark died or afterwards?

   

The second one is about another set of friends wandering through NYC. 

 

When reading the story, one again notices:

  • Arithmetic errors: $12 ÷ 4 = $2; $9 ÷ 4 = $1.50.
  • Biased decisions: dismissing graffiti as laziness; dismissing street musicians as failures; dismissing bakeries as inferior to supermarkets.
  • Misinformation: crossing Central Park takes a day; Empire State Building is tallest in the world; only U.S. subway.
  • Inconsistencies: Amira’s time estimates (half an hour vs. ten hours); Amira’s “human calculator” confidence vs. repeated wrong answers/guesses.

It is easy to come up with similar doubts for LLM-generated summaries. In particular, despite recent advancements improving consistency and reducing errors in LLM-generated text, bias remains a concern in automated summarization, as biased outputs can reinforce existing societal gaps perpetuating unfairness and inequity. This can become critical when LLM-generate summaries are then used for instance to support human decision-making: when the summary fails to represent all groups equally (group fairness), decisions built on it will treat some groups unfairly.

This project is about designing and developing an LLM-powered software agent specifically crafted to mitigate bias in text summarization. To achieve this, you will augment a basic language model with an agentic pipeline that builds upon an ontology.

 

Your first step will be to create an ontology. This ontology will then be used to guide and inform the LLM’s summarization, enabling a more consistent and bias-free output. You are encouraged to experiment with different prompts and approaches for both agent modeling and summarization workflows, and for integrating the ontology withing the LLM agent.

The aim of this project is to demonstrate the extent to which your LLM agent can effectively mitigate bias in text summarization by leveraging structured ontological knowledge, compared to a basic language model that does not leverage an ontology. While your agent is not required to detect every conceivable error type or bias, it should attempt to showcase the benefits of an ontology-driven approach.
 

Dataset

You will be using the DivSumm dataset. It is a dataset consisting of dialect-diverse tweets and human-written summaries.

  • Content: Tweets from three social groups – White-aligned (White), Hispanic (Hisp), and African-American (AA) dialects.

  • Input Data - 25 diverse topics, 90 tweets per topic evenly distributed among 3 dialects (30 tweets per group); Total: 2,250 tweets.

  • Abstractive summaries (Summaries Ground truth) - Human-written reference summaries (two annotators were asked to summarize each topic in 5 sentences using their own words).

Problem Description

Give the n sentences composing the set of documents D (tweets) in the dataset, each accompanied by a social attribute g ∈ G (where G is a set of attributes of a social group), the goal is to generate an abstractive summary S consisting of k sentences (k << n, hyperparameter to be tuned) that not only optimizes textual quality and consistency but also minimizes bias in summarization and ensures group fairness. 

  • Pick three different topics from the entire dataset. These constitute three sets of documents D, each composed of n sentences. 
  • Design and implement an ontology (see details below) and an LLM agent that uses it to generate summaries. 
  • Define 2 different summarization user prompts that request the LLM agent to generate the summary.
  • For each topic and for each prompt, generate three summaries to account for variability of responses in LLM generated text.
  • Evaluate the generated summaries (see evaluation details below) against the human-written reference summaries, and against a baseline model that does not use your ontology.

Ontology

The details that need to be captured by your agent can be divided into the following concepts, which will form the basis of your ontology. The following descriptions includes examples of properties and relations to be included in the ontology for each concepts. Make sure to include at least all the concepts mentioned below + 2 additional concepts, with properties and relations, that can better help the LLM agent mitigate bias based on the three specific topics that you selected from the entire dataset.

 [Person] Each person has an age, name, country of origin, and an occupation. One person can have a single occupation.  Any two people can be married to each other as long as they are 18+. No one lives beyond the age of 110. A person can be friends with another person. A person can have character traits; e.g., reserved, or good-hearted. Each of these traits could entail other things; e.g., a reserved person would not talk to others much. Feel free to add other common-sense rules here.

[Company] A company is an organization that operates within a particular industry and is headquartered in a country, though it may also have offices or facilities in multiple locations. A company has a name, a number of employees, and may be public or privately owned. Companies produce one or more products or services and may collaborate or form partnerships with other companies. Companies generate revenue, employ people in different roles, and may invest in research and development to create new products or improve existing ones.

[Country] A country has a name, a capital, a population, an official language (or multiple official languages), and a form of government. A country contains one or more cities, each of which belongs to exactly one country. Every city has certain landmarks (e.g., Empire State Building) and their properties (e.g., height, when it was built). Each city is famous for having certain types of food (e.g., pancakes in Utrecht).  A country may have a president, prime minister, monarch, or other head of state, depending on its system of government. A country recognizes public holidays. Countries can host sporting competitions, cultural events, international summits, and other large-scale events. A country has borders with zero or more neighboring countries, uses one or more currencies, and may belong to international organizations. Citizens belong to a country, although they may also hold citizenship in multiple countries where permitted.

[Topic-specific concept - Sport] Each sport has a name, a set of rules, and a way to determine a winner. A sport can be played by individuals or teams. A player can participate in one or more sports and belongs to at most one team within a league at a time. Teams compete against each other in matches or games, and each match has a score and an outcome (win, loss, or draw). Players are individuals with attributes such as age, position, and skill level, which may influence their performance. Competitions may be organized into leagues or tournaments, with rankings based on match results. Such competitions may have specific rituals.

[Topic-specific concept - Health] Various health conditions (e.g., protein deficiency), symptoms (e.g., fever) or diseases (e.g., flue) are possible. Health conditions and symptoms are organized in a hierarchy (e.g., headache and muscle ache are two types of pain and pain is a type of symptom). Two health conditions are disjoint. The health conditions affect what a user can eat. Users with allergies are not allowed to eat food of that category (e.g., if allergic to flour, all meals that contain flour are out). Some diseases may originate in a particular city and subsequently spread to other regions.

    1. Describe the design of an ontology. Design an ontology on paper: Your ontology should contain the information above as much as possible. 
      • There should be classes and possibly subclasses for each of the above-mentioned concepts.
      • Furthermore, the ontology should include the appropriate relationships between the classes and some instances.

    3. Write ontology queries. Decide on the information needed for your agent to make decisions and formulate them as queries. Write the queries needed to extract these from the ontology. Try your queries in the Protégé first to make sure they return the results that you expect.

  6. Agent Implementation. Develop your agent: In short, your agent program should take two inputs: a dataset of textual information, and a prompt requesting a summary of input, and generate a bias-free summary.
